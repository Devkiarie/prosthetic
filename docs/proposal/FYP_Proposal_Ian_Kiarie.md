## 1. Background

Surface electromyography (sEMG) is the non-invasive measurement of electrical signals generated by skeletal muscle contraction. When a motor neuron fires, it produces an action potential that propagates along muscle fibers, generating a measurable voltage at the skin surface. The resulting sEMG signal has an amplitude of 50 microvolts to 5 millivolts, a useful frequency band of 20 to 500 Hz, and is contaminated by 50 Hz power line interference, motion artifacts below 20 Hz, and thermal noise from electrode-skin interfaces (Prakash et al., 2026).

In myoelectric prosthetic control, surface electrodes on the residual limb detect sEMG signals during voluntary muscle contractions. A signal processing pipeline amplifies, filters, and segments these signals into analysis windows, from which time-domain features are extracted. A classifier maps the feature vector to an intended hand gesture, which drives actuators in the prosthetic device.

Commercial myoelectric prostheses from manufacturers such as Ottobock and Ossur achieve classification accuracy exceeding 95% but cost between $10,000 and $40,000. The control interface electronics alone account for $3,000 to $8,000 of this total. Even research prototypes documented in the literature cost $500 to $1,500 for the electronics subsystem (Molinari et al., 2026), which is prohibitive in developing economies where the average monthly household income is approximately $150.

Three recent developments make a truly low-cost system feasible. First, the ESP32-S3 microcontroller provides dual-core 240 MHz processing, 512 KB SRAM, hardware-accelerated vector operations, BLE 5.0, and TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers support for approximately $5. Second, TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers enables deployment of quantized neural networks on memory-constrained devices. Third, publications from 2025 and 2026 have demonstrated that sEMG classification on ESP32 platforms is achievable (Salgado et al., 2025), proving the concept's technical viability.

## 2. Problem Statement

Globally, an estimated 30 million people require prosthetic or orthotic devices, with sub-Saharan Africa carrying a disproportionate burden at approximately 0.5% population prevalence (WHO Global Report on Assistive Technology, 2022). In Kenya, the National Council for Persons with Disabilities registers over 30,000 individuals requiring upper-limb assistive devices. The Kenyatta National Hospital orthotics department, the largest referral facility in East and Central Africa, fits fewer than 200 powered prosthetic arms per year.

The primary barrier is cost. The sEMG control interface alone costs $3,000 to $8,000 in commercial systems. Low-cost research prototypes remain in the $500 to $1,500 range for the electronics subsystem. No published system achieves multichannel sEMG acquisition, on-device classification, and prosthetic actuation for under $20 in component cost.

This project addresses the following question: Can a complete, 4-channel myoelectric control system, from skin electrode to mechanical grip, be designed, built, and validated for under $20 in component cost using commercially available components and open-source tools?

---

## 3. Main Objective

To design, implement, and validate a low-cost, 4-channel surface electromyography acquisition and gesture classification system on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller, targeting real-time classification of 8 hand gestures with greater than 80% accuracy and less than 50 millisecond inference latency, with a total electronics bill of materials under $20, driving a 2-DOF prosthetic gripper demonstrator.

---

## 4. Specific Objectives

1. To design and fabricate a 4-channel analog front-end using INA128 instrumentation amplifiers, Sallen-Key bandpass filters (20 to 500 Hz), and twin-T 50 Hz notch filters on a custom 4-layer PCB, achieving a signal-to-noise ratio greater than 20 dB and inter-channel crosstalk less than -40 dB.

2. To implement a real-time digital signal processing pipeline on the ESP32-S3 for 4-channel sEMG feature extraction at 2 kHz sampling rate, computing 24 time-domain features (6 per channel) within 25 milliseconds per 250-millisecond analysis window.

3. To train a 1D convolutional neural network on the NinaPro DB5 public dataset for 8-class gesture classification, quantize it to INT8 format under 100 KB, and deploy it on the ESP32-S3 via TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers, achieving an offline macro F1 score greater than 0.85.

4. To implement a BLE-based user calibration protocol that enables personalized gesture recognition through transfer learning in under 3 minutes, improving cross-user accuracy by at least 10 percentage points.

5. To integrate the classified gesture output with a PCA9685-driven 2-DOF 3D-printed prosthetic gripper and validate the complete system with 5 test subjects, achieving a mean real-time accuracy greater than 80%.

## 5. Related Studies

### 5.1 Single-Channel sEMG on ESP32

Salgado et al. (2025) demonstrated hand gesture classification using a single sEMG channel on an ESP32 microcontroller, classifying 4 gestures with 92% accuracy. Their system validated that the ESP32 ADC is adequate for sEMG when properly conditioned and that inference latency under 10 milliseconds is achievable. However, their work was limited to a single channel, which severely restricts the number of distinguishable gestures, and no prosthetic integration was demonstrated.

### 5.2 Deep Learning for sEMG Recognition

Mekruksavanich et al. (2026) proposed a deep residual network with channel attention for sEMG gesture recognition, achieving 97% accuracy on the NinaPro DB5 dataset. Their architecture uses 1D convolutional layers with residual connections and squeeze-and-excitation attention blocks for 8 or more gesture classes. However, the model is designed for desktop GPU inference with no embedded deployment, making it impractical for a standalone wearable device.

### 5.3 Cross-User Generalization

Lee et al. (2025) addressed cross-user variability in sEMG signals through federated prototype learning. Their FedEMG framework achieves personalization with minimal calibration data (approximately 2 minutes per new user) and resource-efficient model adaptation suitable for edge devices. A key finding is that transfer learning with fine-tuning of only the last classification layer is sufficient for user adaptation, which this project adopts.

### 5.4 Wearable Real-Time Platform

Molinari et al. (2026) built a wearable platform for real-time prosthetic hand control using high-density EMG with 64 channels. Their system achieves real-time control but uses a custom ASIC for the analog front-end and an FPGA for signal processing, with electronics costs exceeding $500. This demonstrates the accuracy achievable with dense electrode arrays but at a cost point inaccessible to the target population.

### 5.5 Modular Low-Cost Prosthetic

Furtado et al. (2026) presented a modular low-cost myoelectric prosthetic hand at the IEEE International Forum on Electrical Engineering. Their design integrates sEMG sensing with actuation using modular finger segments. A key finding is that mechanical simplicity (2 to 3 degrees of freedom) is sufficient for 80% of activities of daily living, which supports this project's 2-DOF gripper approach.

### 5.6 Comprehensive Reviews

Prakash et al. (2026) and Abdikenov et al. (2025) provide surveys of prosthetic control approaches. Consensus findings relevant to this project include: 4 to 8 channels is the optimal trade-off between accuracy and complexity; time-domain features (MAV, RMS, WL, ZC) remain competitive with deep learning features for 6 to 8 gestures; 250 milliseconds is the accepted analysis window length; and CNN-based classifiers outperform SVM and LDA for more than 4 gesture classes.

## 6. Methodology

### 6.1 Signal Specification

The system captures surface electromyography signals with the specifications in Table 2.

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Signal type | Surface EMG, differential measurement |
| Channels | 4 (forearm: flexor carpi radialis, extensor digitorum, flexor digitorum superficialis, brachioradialis) |
| Amplitude range | 50 microvolts to 5 mV (raw), amplified to 25 mV to 2.5 V |
| Frequency band | 20 to 500 Hz, with 50 Hz notch rejection |
| Sampling rate | 2 kHz per channel |
| Analysis window | 250 ms (500 samples), 50 ms step (80% overlap) |
| Features per channel | MAV, RMS, WL, ZC, SSC, Variance (6 features) |
| Total feature vector | 24 elements (6 features times 4 channels) |

### 6.2 Gesture Classes

The system targets 8 hand gestures (Table 3), selected for functional relevance to activities of daily living.

| Class | Gesture | Functional Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Rest | Baseline, no actuation |
| 1 | Open hand | Release objects |
| 2 | Power grasp (closed fist) | Grip objects |
| 3 | Pinch grip | Fine manipulation |
| 4 | Point (index extension) | Directional control |
| 5 | Wrist flexion | Repositioning |
| 6 | Wrist extension | Repositioning |
| 7 | Thumbs up | Social gesture, mode select |

### 6.3 Performance Targets

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Offline F1 score (NinaPro DB5) | Greater than or equal to 0.85 macro |
| On-device real-time accuracy (5 subjects) | Greater than or equal to 80% |
| Inference latency (end-to-end) | Less than 50 ms |
| Model size (INT8 TFLite) | Less than 100 KB |
| System total hardware cost (BOM) | Less than USD 20 |

### 6.4 System Architecture

The system comprises six independently testable modules:

**Module 1: Analog Front-End.** Four channels of INA128 instrumentation amplifiers (gain = 50), followed by Sallen-Key 2nd-order bandpass filters (20 to 500 Hz), twin-T notch filters (50 Hz, greater than 30 dB rejection), and variable gain stages (4x to 10x). Total system gain: 200x to 500x.

**Module 2: Custom PCB.** A 4-layer PCB with separated analog and digital ground planes, designed in KiCad, fabricated by JLCPCB. Analog channels are laid out symmetrically with guard traces and matched differential input paths.

**Module 3: Sampling and DSP Firmware.** An ESP32-S3 running FreeRTOS samples all 4 channels at 2 kHz via DMA. A 250-ms sliding window extracts 24 time-domain features (MAV, RMS, WL, ZC, SSC, Variance per channel).

**Module 4: Gesture Classifier.** A 1D-CNN trained on NinaPro DB5 (10 subjects, 8 gestures), quantized to INT8 (less than 100 KB), and deployed via TFLite Micro on the ESP32-S3.

**Module 5: Actuator Interface.** A PCA9685 I2C PWM driver controls 2 MG996R servos on a 3D-printed 2-DOF gripper. Confidence filtering and debounce logic prevent erratic actuation.

**Module 6: BLE Calibration App.** A BLE-connected mobile application guides new users through a 3-minute calibration session (8 gestures, 3 repetitions each). Transfer learning fine-tunes the final classification layer for the specific user.

### 6.5 Methodology Phases

**Phase 1: Literature Review and Simulation.** Simulate all analog stages using ngspice. Verify filter responses, CMRR, and gain budget. Run Monte Carlo tolerance analysis. Download and explore NinaPro DB5.

**Phase 2: Single-Channel Proof of Concept.** Build one complete analog channel on breadboard. Validate SNR greater than 20 dB, bandpass response, and 50 Hz rejection with oscilloscope measurements.

**Phase 3: PCB Design and Fabrication.** Design 4-channel PCB in KiCad with hierarchical schematics. Fabricate via JLCPCB. Assemble and validate all 4 channels, including inter-channel crosstalk measurement.

**Phase 4: ML Model Training and Deployment.** Extract features from NinaPro DB5. Train baseline classifiers (LDA, SVM, RF) and the target 1D-CNN. Quantize to INT8. Deploy on ESP32-S3. Validate against Python reference.

**Phase 5: Integration.** Wire complete pipeline: electrodes to PCB to ESP32 to classifier to servos. Implement BLE service. Build calibration protocol. Implement decision logic with confidence filtering.

**Phase 6: Validation.** Measure all engineering metrics. Conduct 5-subject experiment with informed consent. Collect per-subject accuracy, F1, latency, and satisfaction data.

### 6.6 Cost Demonstration

The low-cost claim is central to this project. Three cost tiers will be reported:

| Tier | Contents | Target |
|---|---|---|
| A: Electronics BOM | ICs, passives, connectors | Less than $20 |
| B: Prototype BOM | Tier A + PCB, 3D printing, batteries, servos | Less than $65 |
| C: Volume (100+ units) | Tier A at volume pricing | Less than $15 |

A comparison table against published systems and commercial products will demonstrate the cost advantage quantitatively.

### 6.7 End Demonstration

The final deliverable is a live, untethered demonstration:

1. A volunteer places 4 Ag/AgCl surface electrodes on their forearm (less than 3 minutes setup assisted by a BLE mobile app).
2. The system calibrates to the volunteer through a guided 3-minute session.
3. The volunteer performs each of 8 hand gestures; the ESP32-S3 acquires, filters, extracts features, and classifies each gesture in real time.
4. A 3D-printed 2-DOF gripper responds to each classified gesture with the corresponding grip motion.
5. The BLE mobile app displays live gesture predictions with confidence scores.
6. The entire system is powered from batteries with no USB or PC connection.
